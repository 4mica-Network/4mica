At AI Engineer World's Fair, Jan Čurn gave a talk titled
[x402 isn't good (yet)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h6mi88VrPtQ). It is
the most useful critique of the protocol so far, because it comes from running
x402 in front of twenty thousand Actors rather than from a whitepaper. This is
a reply with code attached: two of the three answered outright, and a proposal
for the third.

## His three problems

**Double-spend.** Between the moment a buyer signs a payment and the moment it
settles, nothing stops the same funds from backing a thousand other
signatures. The seller does the work and finds out later.

**401 versus 402.** MCP authentication wants a 401 first, x402 wants a 402.
Servers end up split into one hostname per payment protocol, which he called
twenty different Amazons.

**Metered billing.** x402 was built for fixed prices. An Actor run lasts
seconds or hours and its cost is known at the end. The `upto` scheme arrived
about half a year after it was promised, and it still double-spends. The
workaround Apify ships today is to charge the maximum, do the work and refund
the rest: two transactions per job, and the buyer has to trust the seller to
send the second one.

## What a cryptographic payment guarantee changes

The `4mica-credit` scheme replaces the transfer authorization with a signed
guarantee against collateral the buyer has already deposited with 4Mica core.

**Lock before work.** Each request carries a guarantee for a cap. Core locks
that amount from the buyer's free collateral at issuance, before the seller
starts. Two signatures cannot lock the same dollar, so the thousand-signature
attack fails at the facilitator instead of in the seller's ledger.

**Same host, same header.** `4mica-credit` is one more entry in the `accepts`
array of an ordinary 402, verified and settled through a facilitator that
serves the standard `/verify`, `/settle` and `/supported`. The entry carries
its EIP-712 domain in `extra`, as Apify's `exact` entry carries its name and
version, so a client signs from the 402 and a key and never calls 4Mica. It
adds no host, and it does not solve the 401 versus 402 conflict either,
because that is decided before any scheme is visible. Inside MCP the conflict
is already routed around: Apify's server puts the accepts array in each paid
tool's `_meta`, and the scheme rides in it unchanged. On the plain API, the
header-only fix he asked for exists in principle: a 401 can carry an OAuth
challenge and an MPP-style `WWW-Authenticate: Payment` challenge side by side,
and a guarantee is indifferent to which encoding advertises it.

**Cap at signing, exact at the end.** The buyer signs once, for the cap. When
the run ends, the seller signs a guarantee back to the buyer for the unused
part, under the same domain the cap advertised, and returns it with the
result. The buyer holds a collateral-backed refund the moment the response
arrives, not a promise of a second transaction. When the cycle commits, core
nets the two guarantees to what the run cost. Nothing expires, nothing is held
for fourteen days, and nothing moves on-chain per run. Guarantees then net once
per cycle into one on-chain movement per participant: agents paying Apify,
Apify paying developers, Actors calling Actors, all in the same netting.

## What batch-settlement gives, and what it does not

The talk pointed at Coinbase's
[batch-settlement](https://docs.x402.org/schemes/batch-settlement) as the way
out, and it is a real answer: the buyer escrows once, signs cumulative vouchers
off-chain, and the server claims them in batches with a settlement override
for dynamic prices. That closes double-spend and metering.

Its channel is between one payer and one receiver. A buyer that uses fifty
sellers funds fifty escrows, and every seller runs a channel manager, a
receiver signer and channel storage. With a collateral-backed guarantee the
buyer's deposit is one position usable at every seller, the seller runs
nothing beyond the facilitator calls it already makes, and settlement is one
net position per participant per cycle. There is also no approve transaction
and no ETH float, which `upto` needs for its Permit2 allowance.

## What is not there yet

**Change from the seller's own pocket.** Today the refund guarantee locks that
much of the seller's free collateral until the cycle nets, so a seller needs a
float roughly equal to the unused caps in a cycle. The next core release
removes the float: core will count the guarantees a wallet holds as collateral
for the guarantees it signs. A seller holding a 1 USDC guarantee from a buyer
can sign up to 1 USDC back against it, no deposit needed, and netting
collapses the pair to what the run cost. The refund is then the cap's own
backing returned, and the seller's balance never enters. Two rules keep it
safe: only finalized incoming guarantees count, never ones still pending
validation, and a guarantee drawn on a receivable has to land in the same
cycle as the receivable. A run the seller never settles costs the buyer
nothing, because the cap is only issued at settlement, after the run.

The trust model is the same as `upto`: the seller settles any amount up to
the cap the buyer authorized. The evidence and dispute semantics on top of
that are the conversation we would rather have with the team that owns
Apify's ledger than settle on our own.

**Settlement latency.** Net positions settle about a day after cycle close.
Cycle length is per deployment; daily is the default.
